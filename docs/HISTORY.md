# PerfectPower in the history of perfect powers

*An essay for newcomers. The repository facts in it (the status table and the passages that cite
theorems) are kept current with each release: see `docs/RELEASE_CHECK.md` for the commit they
describe.*

**Where things stand.** Every claim in the repository carries one of four labels, and they are
never added together:

| Label | What it means | Count today |
|---|---|---|
| Lean theorem, no added premise | the kernel checked the whole argument | 72 positive-`k` Mordell curves `y² = x³ + k`, `k ≤ 100`, with complete integral-point lists, among other theorems |
| Lean theorem, conditional on Matveev | complete if a named instance of Matveev's lower bound for linear forms in logarithms is supplied | 24 theorems (negative `k`) |
| Independent agreement (OEIS, census) | exact arithmetic or an independent data source agrees; not a completeness proof | 45 further positive-`k` curves; all 100 positive-`k` rows agree with seven OEIS definitions |
| Accepted downstream proof session | a native tool (Why3, GNATprove) accepted a session using a PerfectPower certificate | see `docs/ARITHMETIC_WORKFLOW.md` |

---

A perfect power is a whole number made by raising another whole number to an exponent of at least two. The number $8$ is a perfect power because it is $2^3$; $9$ is one because it is $3^2$. This is an old idea. What became a modern research problem was asking how often powers can come unusually close to one another, or appear inside expressions whose values are usually ordinary integers. PerfectPower belongs to that second tradition. Its central question is easy to say: **if we put whole numbers into a formula, when does the answer happen to be a square, cube, or higher power?** The repository tries to answer that question with mathematics that a computer can check, while preserving the distinction between a proved answer, a result that still depends on an outside theorem, and an interesting numerical observation.

S. S. Pillai gave one of the great versions of the question its modern shape. Imagine writing all positive perfect powers in increasing order. Near the beginning, $8$ and $9$ sit next to each other. Farther out, might perfect powers keep coming within a fixed distance of one another? Pillai conjectured that, for any fixed positive gap $k$, there are only finitely many ways to write $a^x-b^y=k$ when both exponents are at least two. Equivalently, the gaps between consecutive perfect powers should eventually become arbitrarily large. Pillai developed these ideas through work in the 1930s and 1940s. The fixed-gap conjecture remains a much broader question than the theorems in this repository. That distinction matters: **PerfectPower does not prove Pillai's conjecture.** It takes specified formulas and works out, case by case or family by family, how their perfect-power values behave. ([Waldschmidt, *Perfect powers: Pillai's works and their developments*](https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~michel.waldschmidt/articles/pdf/PerfectPowers.pdf))

The most famous gap is one. Catalan proposed in 1844 that $8$ and $9$ are the only consecutive positive perfect powers: $3^2-2^3=1$. In 1976, Robert Tijdeman proved that there could be only finitely many consecutive perfect powers and obtained an explicit, though enormous, bound. His argument used the theory of logarithmic forms developed after Alan Baker's work. Mihăilescu later completed the determination of the solutions, establishing that $8$ and $9$ are the only pair. This history shows a recurring difference between two kinds of success. A finiteness theorem tells us that the search must end somewhere; a complete solution tells us exactly what the answers are. Turning the first kind of result into the second can require substantial new mathematics.

The repository repeatedly encounters that same difference on a smaller, more controlled scale. Suppose we test a million inputs and find only three squares. We have found three examples, but we have not established that input number one million and one, or one billion and one, cannot produce another. Even a beautiful pattern in the first thousand answers does not settle the rest. PerfectPower is organized around making the word **complete** mean something exact. A complete list must come with an argument that reaches every possible input, including inputs too large to search directly. Sometimes that argument is an elementary congruence: one of a small number of remainders must occur, and none can work. Sometimes it is an infinite pattern described by a recurrence. Sometimes it reduces the question to points on a curve and needs a deep bound. A computer helps enumerate the finite part, but the reason it is legitimate to stop must be in the proof.

The repository's basic counting question can be written in one line. For a chosen expression $S(n)+k$ and a chosen exponent $d$, let $A(N)$ count the positive integers $n\leq N$ for which $S(n)+k$ is a $d$-th power. This count answers more than "have we seen a hit?" If $A(N)=N$, every input is a hit. If it grows roughly like $\sqrt N$, hits continue forever but become increasingly thin among all inputs. If it grows roughly like $\log N$, they are thinner still. If it eventually stops growing, there are only finitely many. The associated density is the long-run fraction $A(N)/N$, when that fraction has a limit. An infinite set can have density zero, so saying "almost every input misses" does **not** say "there are only finitely many hits." This simple distinction drives much of the project's mathematics and its software design.

Consider a formula that is already visibly a square, such as $S(n)+k=(n+1)^2$. Every eligible input is a hit. At the other extreme, a formula may produce squares only at a few exceptional inputs. Between those cases are structured infinite sets. If we ask when $n+1$ is square, the answer is $n=m^2-1$, so there are about $\sqrt N$ hits up to $N$. This is an example of a radical rate: taking a root describes the inputs directly. The repository develops exact reductions and counting arguments for such families. The value of a counting theorem is that it explains the entire pattern, including its speed, instead of merely printing a long prefix of it.

A different infinite pattern appears when $2n^2+1$ is square. Writing the square as $m^2$ gives $m^2-2n^2=1$, a Pell equation. Its solutions are generated by repeatedly multiplying by a suitable unit associated with $\sqrt2$. The inputs grow rapidly from one solution to the next, which is why the number of hits below $N$ grows on a logarithmic scale. Here an endless set can look nearly finite to someone scanning an ordinary range of integers. PerfectPower formalizes Pell orbits and the counting step that turns rapid growth into a logarithmic hit rate. It also connects several apparently different integer sequences to coordinates of the same underlying orbit. The mathematical gain is compression: many lists become views of one structure, with their offsets and coordinate changes stated explicitly.

The harder finite questions often lead to equations such as $y^2=x^3+k$. This is called a Mordell curve. If our original question asks whether $n^3+k$ is a square, then every hit gives an integer point $(x,y)$ on that curve with $x=n$. A list of the curve's **integral points** therefore settles the perfect-power question for every positive input at once. The curve result is stronger than a list of hits, because it also accounts for negative and zero $x$, which the original input range may exclude. For example, the theorem for $y^2=x^3+2$ gives precisely $(-1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$. Since neither point has positive $x$, it implies that $n^3+2$ is never a square for positive integer $n$. That conclusion concerns infinitely many inputs, yet its final answer is short.

How does one prove such a list complete? For the $k=2$ example, the repository does not simply search farther. It rewrites the problem as an equation in two integers,
$$-u^3-3uv^2-2v^3=1.$$
That cubic expression can be recognized as a norm in a particular cubic arithmetic system. A norm is a way to turn an element of that system into an ordinary integer while respecting multiplication. Since the norm here must be $1$ or $-1$, the element must be a unit, meaning it has a multiplicative inverse in the system. The problem then becomes: describe all units, and determine which of them have the special coordinate shape imposed by $u$ and $v$. This is a striking change of viewpoint. Instead of asking an unbounded two-variable search to find every answer, the proof describes an infinite family of possible answers in one exponent and then proves that only one exponent has the required shape.

The $k=2$ proof carries out both halves (`Plus2.lean`). It proves that the relevant units are powers of one explicit unit, apart from a sign. It then proves that a particular coordinate of such a power can vanish only when the exponent is zero. The second step uses an exact argument with divisibility by $3$, following the spirit of Skolem's method. It covers **every integer exponent**, including negative ones; checking the first several thousand powers would not do that. The surviving source solution is $(u,v)=(-1,0)$. Other arithmetic certificates handle the remaining class of cubic forms, and the result reads back as the two curve points $(-1,\pm1)$. The theorem builds, passes lint, depends only on the standard axioms, and passed a full clean-tree `make release-verify` (commit `58279a9`).

The cubic-form language also explains the larger positive-$k$ achievement. A curve point can be turned into a cubic form with a specified numerical invariant. To show that the method covers **all** curve points, one must show that every form with that invariant is equivalent to one on a finite class list. The repository's reduction proof supplies that bridge: it constructs bounds, moves each form into a finite box, and has Lean check the remaining transports. Separately, reducible cubic forms have a complete solver based on exact factorization and a finite calculation. Together these pieces produced complete integral-point lists for 39 positive-$k$ curves with no class-list premise and no Matveev premise. The $k=2$ source theorem added a fortieth and, more significantly, demonstrated how to handle an **irreducible** source. The achievement is not that forty curves were searched; it is that the arguments explaining why their lists are complete were assembled into machine-checked theorems.

That demonstration has since been turned into an engine (`SkolemP.lean`, `RankOne.lean`). The divisibility argument was proved once for every odd prime: if a unit's multiplication matrix $A$ satisfies $A^M=I+pD$ with $p\nmid D_{20}$, and $p\nmid(A^r)_{20}$ for $0<r<M$, then the corner coordinate of $A^N$ vanishes only at $N=0$. Unit generation was proved once for every cubic order with one real embedding, using a check of between fifteen and about eighty thousand candidates instead of a box search. Each new source now needs only finite, kernel-checked data: a unit, a root bracket, and a prime. With them, 72 positive-$k$ curves have complete lists with no added premise, 33 of them through irreducible sources. Two later steps widened the engine. A nonmonic source with a known point is moved to a monic one by a determinant-one change of variables built from that point. A source with several solutions gets a finite zero set: each residue class of exponents is settled by the Skolem prime, by recentring at a known root, or by a second prime. A nonmonic source with no known point can be excluded through norm representatives and one congruence on every orbit, which settled $k=11$.

For a general reader, "machine checked" means the computer receives not only the proposed answer but a proof built from small steps governed by a formal language. The program may help discover a unit, a useful modulus, a finite box, or a candidate list. The proof checker must then accept the reasoning that makes each discovery sufficient. A Python calculation can report that no solution was found in a large region; Lean needs a justified bound before that region can close an infinite problem. Conversely, a theorem may depend on a clearly named premise that has not been proved inside Lean. The repository makes those premises visible rather than quietly treating them as accomplished facts. Its negative-$k$ work has extensive structural proofs and complete curve results **conditional on Matveev-type analytic bounds**. Those are substantial reductions, but they should not be introduced to a newcomer as unconditional solutions while that premise remains external.

This brings us back to Baker and Tijdeman. Linear forms in logarithms are a way of showing that certain algebraic quantities cannot be *too* close together unless a special relation holds. Such lower bounds can turn an apparently endless search into one with a finite, explicit ceiling. Matveev's theorem is a later powerful result in this tradition, and the repository uses named versions of its bounds as the remaining analytic input for several negative-$k$ arguments. The surrounding algebra, unit calculations, norm representatives, descent steps, and finite checks can already be formalized without pretending that this difficult external theorem has been formalized too. The connection to the conventional history is direct: effective logarithmic bounds helped transform finiteness into computation in classic exponential equations, and here they mark a precise boundary between a conditional complete list and a fully internal proof.

There is another reason the project cares about exact lists and exact rates. A density of zero can conceal radically different experiences for a user or a program. The square inputs in $n+1$ never end and occur on a square-root scale. Pell inputs never end and occur on a logarithmic scale. A finite curve has a last hit. All three have density zero. A service that only reports "rare" has lost the information needed to decide whether to keep searching, generate the next answer directly, or stop with a certificate. PerfectPower's classification aims to return the structural explanation alongside the count. In favorable cases it can replace expensive repeated testing with a formula for the hits, an orbit that generates them, or a proof that the listed hits are all there are.

The repository also studies what happens when a hit set is viewed through a mathematical transform. Imagine assigning each hit a weight that decreases as its input grows and adding the weights. A heat transform uses a weight such as $e^{-tn}$; a Dirichlet series uses $n^{-s}$. These are different ways of viewing the same set at different scales. When the number of hits below $N$ follows a sufficiently precise power-and-logarithm law, the repository has formalized results that turn that count into corresponding normalized limits for the transforms. For a finite hit set, the heat transform approaches the exact number of hits as the weights approach one. This is valuable because a transform can make a distribution easier to analyze, but it must not be asked to say more than its hypotheses justify. Recovering a precise count *from* a transform is a separate converse problem and remains open in the formal development.

The sequence archive has a similarly careful role. The On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences contains many lists that resemble coordinates of Pell orbits or counts of Mordell-curve points. PerfectPower uses those entries to **discover connections and check definitions**. For the positive-$k$ curves, all 100 rows of the project's curve receipt were compared with seven original OEIS entries (`receipts/positive_k_oeis.json`). The entries describe total signed-$y$ solutions, solutions with nonnegative $y$, curves with no points, and numbers of distinct $x$-coordinates. The comparison found zero disagreements. It also checked the easy-to-miss case $y=0$, which contributes one point rather than a positive-and-negative pair. That is a useful regression against an independently written body of data. It does not turn the 28 curves that still rely on census evidence into Lean theorems. The 72 theorem-backed rows and the other 28 rows remain different kinds of knowledge.

This separation is useful outside pure mathematics. Suppose a solver is testing whether a complex arithmetic condition can hold. It might spend a great deal of time asking a general-purpose search engine to rediscover facts that a specialized arithmetic certificate can settle immediately. A certificate could prove that an entire branch is impossible, provide every solution in a finite branch, or replace repeated queries with a direct generator for an infinite family. That is the software idea behind the repository: **insert mathematical structure where it removes work, then make the inserted claim independently checkable**. The project has already explored this idea with arithmetic verification conditions and an unsigned bitvector rule relevant to Why3-style workflows. Its SMT benchmark (134 native-bitvector obligations from Why3's Von Neumann square root) raised the solved obligations from 128 to 130 and lowered the aggregate time. The native-session status is recorded in `docs/ARITHMETIC_WORKFLOW.md`; no GNATprove run is claimed, so those benchmark results establish a promising adapter experiment, not acceptance in an industrial verification pipeline.

Perfect-power detection is also a real algorithmic operation: for instance, the Agrawal–Kayal–Saxena primality algorithm excludes inputs that are nontrivial perfect powers as part of its procedure ([*PRIMES is in P*](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v160-n2-p12.pdf)). That is a concrete example of perfect-power structure helping organize a number-theoretic algorithm. It does **not** mean PerfectPower is a faster cryptographic system, breaks a discrete-logarithm problem, or supplies a replacement for practical factoring software. Its closer prospective application is as a source of exact arithmetic subroutines and certificates for symbolic computation and verification. Those applications would need their own integrations and measured workloads.

The work on polynomial values also has a long conventional lineage. Researchers have studied when a polynomial takes a perfect-power value, often using factorization, Diophantine approximation, recurrence sequences, and logarithmic bounds. Shorey and Tijdeman devote a chapter of their book on exponential Diophantine equations to perfect powers at integral values of a polynomial ([*Exponential Diophantine Equations*, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/exponential-diophantine-equations/perfect-powers-at-integral-values-of-a-polynomial/73750B82E1515E8FD664E23E2C40B4A2)). PerfectPower sits naturally in that subject, but it asks an additional organizational question: can we make the possible behaviors, their counting rates, the complete special-case solutions, and their remaining assumptions legible to both mathematicians and software? A historical paper may establish a powerful theorem for a family; a search program may find examples; a formal library may prove a general reduction. The repository tries to connect those roles without calling them interchangeable.

Its open problems are part of an honest description of what has been built. The positive-$k$ program had 104 locally admissible irreducible cubic equations in its workload receipt; 19 of them now have Lean source theorems. The others are shifted monic sources with large regulators, sources with several solutions, nonmonic sources that need norm representatives, and sources where no small prime satisfies the Skolem conditions (`receipts/rank_one_blockers.json`). On the negative-$k$ side, the Matveev premise remains. Quartic genus-one models and the necessity direction of the Bilu–Tichy classification are further mathematical projects. The software side still needs a native consumer to accept and benefit from the certificates. None of these gaps diminishes an established theorem; each says exactly what work would extend its reach.

The most accessible way to understand the repository, then, is to imagine three questions asked of every perfect-power pattern. **Are there hits? Are there infinitely many? Can we prove that our description accounts for all of them?** Pillai's grand question about gaps asks these at the scale of all perfect powers. PerfectPower asks them for specified formulas and works toward answers that a proof checker and a program can use. Sometimes the answer is "every input." Sometimes it is an infinite but thinning sequence with a predictable growth rate. Sometimes it is a short, complete list, such as the points on $y^2=x^3+2$. Sometimes the honest answer is "complete if this named deep theorem is supplied." Each answer conveys more than a table of successful experiments.

What I find most compelling as a fan of the repository is this movement from a recognizable pattern to an **accountable stopping point**. A perfect power is elementary enough to explain to a child, yet proving that a particular polynomial produces no more of them can take algebraic number theory, careful reductions, and a substantial formal proof. The repository makes that journey visible. It lets a reader see where a Pell orbit replaces search, where a cubic norm turns a curve into a unit problem, where a finite check is licensed by a proved bound, and where a famous analytic theorem is still being assumed. It also shows why these distinctions have practical value: a trustworthy "there are no others" can save work that an unbounded search would never know it could stop doing. That is a meaningful contribution relative to the classical story, without needing to claim that the classical story has been finished.
