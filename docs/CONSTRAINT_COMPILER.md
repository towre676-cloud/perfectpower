# The constraint compiler: from structure to execution

A program often meets an arithmetic requirement that does not mention perfect powers: *this value
must be a triangular number*, *this quadratic must have an integer root*, *this polynomial must be a
square*. The usual answer is to test every candidate. PerfectPower can often do better. Its
classification says, before any search, whether solutions occur at every $n$, along a structured
infinite family, along sparse Pell orbits, or only finitely often. The compiler turns that
classification into an executable plan. Each plan states its outcome, where its justification
comes from, and what it does not know.

```sh
PYTHONPATH=python python3 -m perfectpower solve --expr '64*n**3 - 120*n**2 + 75*n - 16' --form triangular
PYTHONPATH=python python3 -m perfectpower solve --expr '2*n**2 + 1' --program     # the generated program
```

The files are `python/perfectpower/compiler.py` (plans), `python/perfectpower/specialize.py`
(program generation), `PerfectPower/Reduction.lean` (the reduction theorems),
`receipts/constraint_demos.json` (worked examples, regenerated by `make verify`) and
`receipts/constraint_benchmarks.json` (timings, produced by `make bench`).

## 1. Input: three constraint forms

The variable $n$ ranges over integers $n\ge1$. Coefficients are integers.

| Form | Meaning | Witness |
|---|---|---|
| `PowerConstraint(F, d)` | $F(n)=m^d$ for some $m\in\mathbb Z$ | $m$ (both signs for even $d$) |
| `TriangularConstraint(F, dom)` | $y(y+1)/2=F(n)$ for some $y\in$ dom | $y$ |
| `QuadraticRootConstraint(a, b, c, F, dom)` | $ay^2+by+c=F(n)$ for some $y\in$ dom, $a\ne0$ | $y$ |

The domain `dom` of $y$ is $\mathbb Z$ (`int`), $y\ge0$ (`nonneg`) or $y\ge1$ (`pos`). The
specializer (`specialize.py`) takes a brute-force loop over $n$ whose test is one of these forms,
applied to a polynomial written with `+ - * **` and integer literals.

## 2. Exact reductions (Lean: `Reduction.lean`)

A reduction is **exact** when it loses nothing and invents nothing. Its forward map sends
solutions to solutions. Its backward map is *partial*: it is defined exactly on the admissible
reduced solutions, and it inverts the forward map. In Lean this is `Reduction.Exact P Q`, with
`fwd`, `bwd : β → Option α` and three laws. The general theorems are:

- `Exact.iff`: $P(a)\iff\exists b,\ Q(b)\wedge \mathrm{bwd}(b)=a$.
- `Exact.comp`: reductions compose, and their admissibility conditions compose with `Option.bind`.
- `Exact.pull_complete`: **completeness is transported**. A complete finite list of reduced
  solutions pulls back to a complete finite list of original solutions.
- `Exact.count_eq`: for reductions that keep $n$, the hit counts agree after the admissibility
  filter.

The instances are:

| Reduction | Forward | Backward (admissibility) | Lean |
|---|---|---|---|
| affine | $t=rn+s$ | $n=(t-s)/r$ when $r\mid t-s$ and $(t-s)/r\ge1$ | `Reduction.affine`, `Transport.affine_count` |
| quadratic | $m=2ay+b$, $m^2=4aF(n)+b^2-4ac$ | $y=(m-b)/2a$ when $2a\mid m-b$ and $y\in$ dom, trying **both** signs of $m$ | `Reduction.quadratic` |
| triangular | $m=2y+1$, $m^2=8F(n)+1$ | $y=(m-1)/2$: always an integer, since $m$ is odd | `Reduction.triangular`, `triangular_count_int`, `triangular_count_nonneg` |

**A square discriminant alone is not enough.** For $2y^2+y=1$ the discriminant $9$ is a square.
But $y=(3-1)/4$ is not an integer, and $y=(-3-1)/4=-1$ is negative, so there is no solution with
$y\ge0$. The compiler records such a step as `filter_trivial: false`. For triangular numbers the
filter is provably trivial when $y\in\mathbb Z$ or $y\ge0$: `triangular_count` equates the
number of $n\le N$ with $F(n)$ triangular to $A(8F+1)$. With $y\ge1$ it is not trivial, since
$F(n)=0$ has only $y=0,-1$.

**A composite, closed in Lean** (`tri_cube_complete`): with $n\ge1$ and $y\in\mathbb Z$,
$y(y+1)/2=64n^3-120n^2+75n-16$ holds exactly for $(n,y)=(1,2)$ and $(1,-3)$. The proof composes
the triangular reduction (giving $m^2=8F(n)+1=(8n-5)^3-2$) with the affine substitution
$t=8n-5$, pulls back the complete list $\{(3,\pm5)\}$ of `MordellMinus2.points`, and computes the
pull-back by `decide`.

## 3. Output: a plan with one explicit outcome

| Status | Meaning | Operations |
|---|---|---|
| `COMPLETE_FINITE` | a complete finite list of solutions | `all_hits`, `iter_hits`, `count`, `contains` |
| `STRUCTURED_INFINITE` | infinitely many, generated exactly (every $n$, radical parametrization, Pell orbits) | `iter_hits`, `count`, `contains` |
| `STRUCTURED_FILTERED` | an exact generator of the reduced family plus a nontrivial exact admissibility filter; exact to any $N$, but infinitude not decided | `iter_hits`, `count`, `contains` |
| `CLASSIFIED_FINITE` | finite (Siegel via Theorem G), exact to any given $N$, no complete list | `iter_hits`, `count`, `contains` |
| `NOT_ENUMERATED` | finite (Siegel via Theorem G), no effective enumeration here | `contains`, `bounded_evidence` only |

A finiteness theorem never becomes an empty list: a `NOT_ENUMERATED` plan raises `NotEnumerable`
on `iter_hits`, `count` and `all_hits`. `bounded_evidence(N)` scans $1\le n\le N$ and labels the
result `BOUNDED_EVIDENCE`. For example, $n^3+17=m^2$ yields $n=2,4,8,43,52,5234$ up to $10^4$,
with no claim beyond that bound.

**Justification is separate from execution.** A plan lists its justification: Lean theorem names
(the unit tests check that each one is a declaration in `PerfectPower/`), pp-cert/1
certificates checked in Lean, or the Python structural algorithm (tested against scans, not
verified). `execution_verified` is always `false`, because the Python that executes a plan is not
formally verified, whatever the status of the mathematics behind it.

The solver for the reduced constraint $G(n)=m^d$ is chosen in this order:

1. **Transport to a solved Mordell curve.** If $d=2$ and $G(n)=(rn+s)^3+k$ exactly, and
   $y^2=x^3+k$ is solved in Lean, the plan is a linear test. The solved values are
   $k=-2,-4,-5,-6,-13$, $-432u^6$, and the 1163 values of `Generated/MordellDescent.lean`.
2. **Integer Pell quadratic.** If $d=2$ and $G=An^2+Bn+C$ with $A>0$ not a square and
   $\Delta\ne0$, the plan works in $G$'s own coordinates $X=2An+B$ with $X^2-4Am^2=\Delta$. It
   uses the orbit seeds, the transition by the fundamental unit, and the admissibility
   $X\equiv B\pmod{2A}$. Populated vs. unpopulated is decided by the period of the orbit mod $2A$
   (`branch_infinite_iff`).
3. **The atlas** (`atlas.classify`): identity (every $n$), twisted power (zeros only), radical
   parametrization, Pell branches, Runge enumeration (with the committed certificate when one
   exists), exact root reduction, or not enumerated.

## 4. Worked examples (`receipts/constraint_demos.json`)

Each demo records the original loop, the plan, the generated program, and the outputs of both at
$N=10^4$, which must agree. `make verify` regenerates the receipt.

| Demo | Constraint | Plan | Answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| `cube_transport` | $(5n-7)^3-2=m^2$ | transport, `COMPLETE_FINITE` | test `5*n + (-7) == 3`: only $n=2$ |
| `pell_2n2_plus_1` | $2n^2+1=m^2$ | Pell orbits | $n'=3n+2m$, $m'=4n+3m$ from $(0,1)$: $n=2,12,70,408,\dots$ |
| `square_triangular` | $y(y+1)/2=n^2$, $y\ge0$ | triangular → Pell | $(n,y)=(1,1),(6,8),(35,49),(204,288),\dots$ |

| `quadratic_filter` | $2y^2+y=n$, $y\ge0$ | radical + filter, `STRUCTURED_FILTERED` | $n=3,10,21,36,\dots$ ($8n+1$ square *and* $4\mid m-1$) |
| `pell_large_unit` | $991n^2+1=m^2$ | Pell orbits | no hit below $1.2\cdot10^{28}$; the first is $n=12055735790331359447442538767$ |

## 5. Does it pay? (`make bench`, `receipts/constraint_benchmarks.json`)

These timings come from one machine. They vary between runs and machines, and the receipt records
the platform. The brute-force baseline is the plain Python loop that the specializer writes: an
exact integer root per $n$, with no sieve. It is measured up to $N=10^5$ only.

| Demo | Preprocessing | Brute force at $10^5$ | Specialized at $10^5$ | Specialized at $10^{30}$ | Crossover |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `cube_transport` | 0.07 ms | 0.37 s | 2 µs | 2 µs (1 hit) | $\le10^3$ |
| `pell_2n2_plus_1` | 0.3 ms | 0.23 s | 34 µs | 1 ms (39 hits) | $\le10^3$ |
| `square_triangular` | 0.5 ms | 17 ms | 24 µs | 0.6 ms (40 hits) | $10^4$ |
| `quadratic_filter` | 0.5 ms | 10 ms | 1.7 ms | 0.32 s at $10^9$ (22360 hits) | $10^4$ |
| `pell_large_unit` | 0.5 ms | 0.30 s | 3 µs | 65 µs (1 hit) | $\le10^3$ |
| `ljunggren` | 2.2 ms | 0.58 s | 2 µs | 2 µs (1 hit) | $\le10^3$ |

What the measurements show:

- For small $N$ the loop can win. The square-triangular loop is cheap per step, and preprocessing
  (fundamental unit, orbit seeds, code generation) costs about 0.5 ms. The crossover is observed
  between $10^3$ and $10^4$.
- For Pell and finite plans, the specialized cost grows with the number of *hits*, which is
  logarithmic or constant. Brute force grows with $N$.
- Radical families are dense ($\asymp N^{1/t}$). There the output itself dominates, and at
  $N=10^{12}$ the plan must list about 707,000 solutions (17 s).
- Preprocessing is not always cheap. Finding a fundamental unit can take up to a
  continued-fraction period, loading the 1163-curve registry costs about 2 ms once, and Runge
  enumeration depends on the certificate's threshold. The atlas's own Pell data for $991n^2+1$
  rescales the quadratic to $A\approx991^3$, and its period loop would not finish. The compiler
  avoids that by using the quadratic's own coordinates. The atlas is unchanged.

## 6. Correctness checks

- `python/tests/test_compiler.py`:
  - reduction pitfalls: a square discriminant with an inadmissible root, both signs, the $y\ge1$
    domain, zero values, negative values, cube roots of negatives;
  - transport, including an argument outside the image;
  - the composite example and the far Pell hit;
  - `NOT_ENUMERATED` refusing to enumerate;
  - every cited Lean name exists;
  - a seeded differential test of 300 random loops. Plan, `contains`, `count`, the generated
    program and the brute-force loop must all agree.
- During development, 1500 random constraints and 800 random loops (558 of them specialized)
  agreed with brute force, with 0 mismatches.

**A completeness bug, found in review and fixed.** Release `ddbdcb8` froze a *bounded* Pell
branch ($A<0$, or $A$ a square) by scanning only to $n\le10^6$. Two consequences followed:
- for $F(n)=((n-1000007)^2-2)^2$ with $d=4$, `contains(1000008)` found a hit that `all_hits()`,
  labelled `COMPLETE_FINITE`, omitted;
- the generated program for $(2(n-1000007)^2-1)^2=m^4$ lost its hit at $n=1000007$.

The fix derives the search limit from the Lean bounds `hit_le_of_neg` and `hit_le_of_square`, and
for $A<0$ it scans only the interval where the quadratic is nonnegative. The regression tests
(`FarHits`) fail on `ddbdcb8` and pass now. One of them shifts random finite families by
$10^6+7$ and compares every `COMPLETE_FINITE` list with brute force around the shift. A larger
far-shift fuzz (3000 constraints) found no incomplete list. It did show that a Runge plan whose
scan prefix is too long raised an error; such a plan is now `NOT_ENUMERATED` with the reason.

## 7. The effectiveness boundary

What remains open is exactly the atlas's open part:

- `NOT_ENUMERATED` covers the finite type outside Runge and outside the solved Mordell registry
  (for example $n^3+17$). Siegel gives finiteness but no bound. Baker-type bounds would make these
  effective. They are not implemented, and no plan pretends otherwise.
- `STRUCTURED_FILTERED` plans are exact to any $N$. Whether infinitely many solutions survive a
  nontrivial divisibility filter is not decided here. For Pell families the filter is periodic
  along each orbit, so a period argument like `branch_infinite_iff` would decide it. That is the
  next step.
- Only the reductions of §2 are proved in Lean. The Python code that recognizes them and runs the
  plans is tested, not verified.
