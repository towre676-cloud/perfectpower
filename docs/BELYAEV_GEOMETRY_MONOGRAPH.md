# Exact geometry from incidence to usable spatial structure

Oleg A. Belyaev's [Fundamentals of Geometry](https://polly.phys.msu.ru/~belyaev/geometry.pdf), dated February 28, 2007, is useful here because it separates incidence, betweenness, polygon structure, congruence and symmetry. The source's discussions of paths and simplicity, printed pages 69–72, and isometries, printed pages 162–167, motivate explicit distinctions in this release: closed versus open objects, boundary contact versus interior penetration, and rigid congruence versus coordinate resemblance. The retained literature receipt maps the selected source sections to our modules. Ear triangulation, determinant predicates, visibility routing and mesh transport are engineering extensions using established coordinate mathematics; they are not presented as algorithms copied from the book.

**What this release adds.** PerfectPower's previous Euclidean layer already had exact segment intersection, segment-to-point distance, disk contact, winding information and bounded nearest-site cells. Those primitives now connect to polygon validation, triangle decomposition, exact areas and centroids, rational rigid maps, circle predicates, Voronoi triangle meshes and obstacle routing. Each operation has a JSON command and reproducible examples. This is a rational-coordinate Euclidean implementation. It does not formalize all Hilbert axioms, and the rational plane does not satisfy real-number completeness. Hyperbolic and projective geometry are not added by this release.

**Incidence and betweenness.** Two distinct rational points determine a line. Its coefficients are cleared to primitive integers, with a fixed sign convention, so reversing the input points gives the same normalized representation. The equation is \(Ax+By+C=0\). Two valid lines are classified as meeting at one exact rational point, parallel, or coincident. Coincident points cannot silently define a line. Strict betweenness excludes endpoints; closed segment membership includes them. These conventions make zero-length segments and endpoint contacts explicit rather than relying on an epsilon.

**Polygon admission before computation.** A polygon may be supplied with or without its first vertex repeated at the end. The single closing repetition is removed, then the routine checks all side pairs. Adjacent sides may meet at their common endpoint. Nonadjacent contact, overlap, crossing, repeated vertices, zero-length sides and zero area prevent admission as a simple polygon. Consecutive collinear vertices are allowed when their sides merely continue through their common endpoint. Backtracking along an edge is rejected as overlap. The structural report retains the actual offending side indices and intersection data, making a failed input inspectable.

This admission stage matters for downstream applications. A self-crossing path can have a signed area or winding number, but that does not make it a simple filled obstacle. Routing and triangulation require the latter meaning. The release does not reinterpret invalid obstacle data as an empty or harmless polygon.

**Exact triangle decomposition.** Validated simple polygons without holes are normalized to counterclockwise order. Redundant collinear vertices are removed only when they lie between their neighbours. Ear clipping then chooses a strictly positive-area corner whose closed triangle contains no other retained polygon vertex. All coordinates remain rational; no new approximate vertex is introduced. The result records the normalized vertices, removed vertices, triangles and exact areas. It checks positivity and agreement of the triangle-area sum with the polygon area.

These checks are accompanied by independent grid tests comparing triangle membership with a winding-based point-location routine. The tests also check that strict triangle interiors do not overlap at sampled points. They are meaningful execution tests, not a general formal proof of the clipping algorithm. In particular, area equality alone would not establish coverage or disjointness. The program's triangulation algorithm remains tested Python.

For the concave polygon \((0,0),(4,0),(4,4),(2,2),(0,4)\), the retained decomposition has three triangles of area four each, for total area twelve. The centroid calculation uses the signed boundary formula and remains invariant when the vertex order is reversed. It represents a uniform filled lamina; for a concave shape its centroid need not lie inside the polygon. Point-location queries distinguish `INSIDE`, `BOUNDARY` and `OUTSIDE` exactly.

**Circle predicates without square roots.** A noncollinear rational triangle has a rational circumcenter and a rational squared circumradius. The routine solves the two perpendicular-bisector equations exactly. It rejects a collinear triangle, whose circumcircle is not uniquely determined. The incircle predicate evaluates a translated determinant and corrects its sign for the orientation of the three defining sites. Reversing their order therefore leaves the geometric inside/outside classification unchanged. Cocircular points receive the separate result `ON`.

The retained precision example uses \(N=2^{60}\), the triangle \((0,0),(N,0),(0,N)\), and query point \((N,N-1)\). Exact arithmetic classifies the query as inside the circumcircle. In the recorded binary64 calculation, \(N-1\) rounds to \(N\), yielding a boundary classification. This is one adversarial example demonstrating a real predicate distinction, not a general error-rate benchmark. Such distinctions can affect mesh decisions and nearest-site topology.

**Recovering congruence as an actual map.** For labelled source and target point lists, the routine finds two distinct source points and constructs the two candidate rational orthogonal maps taking their directed separation to the target separation: one orientation preserving and one reversing. It then checks every supplied correspondence exactly. The result may contain both maps for collinear data, one map for a noncollinear configuration, or no map. If the source contains only one distinct point and the target does too, the result is explicitly underdetermined. A translation example is returned, while infinitely many possible rigid maps are not falsely represented by a finite complete list.

A general transformation is represented by a two-by-two rational orthogonal matrix \(R\) and rational translation \(t\), with \(p\mapsto Rp+t\). Application checks orthogonality and any supplied orientation label. Floats and nonorthogonal scaling matrices are rejected. The retained rotation and reflection use coefficients \(3/5\) and \(4/5\), with translation \((7/3,-2/7)\). They are substantial rational maps rather than only integer-axis examples.

**What the matrix proofs establish.** `PerfectPower/GeometryFoundations.lean` proves the quadratic norm expansion for an arbitrary integer matrix, its norm-scaling consequence under orthogonality conditions, determinant scaling, a midpoint order fact and the triangle signed-area identity. The generated sources clear the rational matrix and translation denominators to a positive integer \(D\). If \(M=DR\), they prove

\[
 \|M(p-q)\|^2=D^2\|p-q\|^2,
 \qquad \det(Mu,Mv)=\varepsilon D^2\det(u,v),
\]

for every integer input pair or vector pair, with \(\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}\). The affine-distance statement includes the cleared translation and proves that it cancels from differences. These are universal algebraic statements for the supplied map, not checks at the three demonstration points. Their rational-coordinate interpretation uses denominator clearing. The retained rotation and reflection sources both pass Lean.

A third emitted source checks the actual integer-scaled triangle determinants, their positivity, the source polygon's area and the exact area sum. Its scope is numerical area evidence, not a theorem that the Python triangulation covers the polygon. The compiler metadata begins with `kernel_checked: false`; a separate successful-run receipt binds the exact checked source hashes. Corrupting a matrix condition or changing an area total causes Lean to reject the source. Accepted proofs use only standard Lean axioms, and the accepted log has no `sorryAx`.

**Turning Voronoi cells into mesh data.** The existing half-plane clipping routine supplies the bounded Euclidean cells. The new adapter attaches triangles, exact areas and exact centroids to full-dimensional cells. Empty, point and segment cells receive explicit dimension labels and no fictitious positive area. Coincident site labels retain their ties and share the same geometry. The total area counts a coincident site's geometry once and is checked against the declared rectangular domain's area. This check is a useful replay invariant, not by itself a formal tiling theorem.

The transport operation takes a canonical diagram and a rigid map. It transforms sites, cell vertices, inequalities and the original domain boundary. If a cell inequality is \(n\cdot x\le b\), its image is

\[
 (Rn)\cdot x'\le b+(Rn)\cdot t.
\]

A rotated source rectangle becomes a rotated quadrilateral; the output retains that actual boundary rather than pretending it is an axis-aligned box. Tests check preserved nearest-site labels, cell areas and every transformed vertex's inequalities. This gives downstream code a way to move a scene's exact geometry without rerunning nearest-cell discovery. The matrix proofs supply its algebraic foundation; the diagram-transport implementation itself remains tested Python.

**Routing with an explicit objective.** Polygon interiors are forbidden, while boundary contact and movement along an edge are allowed. Segment visibility is decided by splitting a candidate at all exact intersections with each polygon boundary and testing an interior point of each resulting interval. Testing only a midpoint of the original segment would miss some crossings of concave shapes. Endpoints inside an obstacle are rejected. Overlapping obstacles are permitted, with each interior tested separately.

The router builds the full visibility graph whose vertices are the supplied endpoints and distinct obstacle vertices. An edge exists exactly when its straight segment passes the visibility test. Its weight is the rational Manhattan length \(|\Delta x|+|\Delta y|\). Dijkstra's algorithm chooses the minimum sum, resolving ties by fewer edges and then lexicographic vertex indices. The result includes the graph, path, exact cost and work count. `UNREACHABLE` means unreachable in this specified graph. It does not claim that arbitrary intermediate bend points were searched.

For the square obstacle \([0,2]\times[0,2]\), endpoints \((-1,1)\) and \((3,1)\) cannot be joined by the direct segment. The retained path travels through \((0,0)\) and \((2,0)\), with Manhattan cost six. Each returned segment is independently checked for visibility. A second shortest-path implementation using repeated relaxation agrees with the saved graph costs. An overlapping obstacle ring also exercises an unreachable component.

This is an exact finite-graph routing application, not a globally shortest Euclidean string path. It supplies zero-clearance geometry: tangency and boundary travel are allowed. A physical string needs radius, tension, friction, collision response and dynamics. This release provides backend geometry that can support such work; it does not rebuild or certify the separate Windows STRING executable. Manhattan cost also depends on the coordinate axes, so Euclidean rigid transformations preserve admissibility and geometry but need not preserve this routing objective.

**Reproduction and practical boundaries.** Run `PYTHONPATH=python python3 -m perfectpower geometry-foundations triangulate examples/belyaev_geometry/triangulate.json`, or choose `route`, `congruence`, `voronoi-mesh` or `transport-voronoi` with the corresponding example. `make geometry-foundations` runs fourteen new test methods and regenerates receipts. `make geometry-foundations-kernel` checks the foundations, three generated sources and rejection cases using Lean 4.20.0. The command honors `PERFECTPOWER_LEAN` when the executable needs an explicit path. Windows users can set `PYTHONPATH` to the repository's `python` directory and use their installed Python launcher.

The targeted release run also passes fourteen Euclidean, nine linear-product and seventeen graph-method regression tests: fifty-four tests in total. Tests cover rational transformations, reflection ambiguity, degenerate inputs, random circumcircles, sampled triangle partitions, duplicate nearest-site labels, route optimum comparison, unreachable components, packet tampering and resource limits. Work budgets raise rather than returning a truncated object as complete. Their counts track stated algorithm stages and predicate calls; they are not uniform bit-complexity or wall-clock guarantees. The router additionally limits graph vertices, and Voronoi construction has a separate site-pair limit.

The value of the release is the connection between previously isolated exact predicates and useful spatial objects. A polygon becomes an inspectable obstacle or triangle mesh; a labelled correspondence becomes a verified distance-preserving transformation; a nearest-site diagram can be meshed and transported; and a string-like path can be routed around supplied polygon interiors with a precise optimization contract. The remaining mathematical work includes formal triangulation coverage, formally verified graph routing, positive-clearance geometry, and any future hyperbolic or projective models. These are explicit next steps rather than capabilities inferred from a book's title.
